> Use this skill to turn a Notion page into a simple, visual presentation that still works as a normal document. The page should be easy to scroll, easy to speak from, and easy to understand without dense slide design.

## Core Idea

Build presentations as a sequence of clear sections separated by dividers.

**Each divider acts like a slide break.** Everything between two dividers should communicate one main idea: a question, agenda item, visual reference, proposal component, timeline, role, or next step.

This follows the simple meeting flow and the visual, section-by-section structure of Brian's own reference pages in Notion.

## Presentation Principles

- One main idea per section.
- Separate every major idea with a divider.
- Keep headings short enough to read instantly.
- Prefer one sentence, 3–6 bullets, one callout, or one strong visual over a paragraph.
- Use images, video, bookmarks, and embeds as presentation content—not decoration.
- Keep the main path simple. Put research, raw notes, prompts, and backup detail inside toggles or at the end.
- Leave whitespace. Do not fill every section simply because space is available.
- Make the page understandable while scrolling, but avoid writing a complete script onto the page.

## Dividers

Use `---` between presentation sections.

A divider should usually appear:

- after the opening/title section;
- between agenda topics;
- before a major question or proposal component;
- before and after a full-width image, video, or embed when it deserves its own moment;
- between context, process, output, and next steps;
- before the closing ask or discussion.

Do not add dividers between small bullets that belong to the same thought. The divider marks a change in what the presenter is talking about.

## Images and Media

Images should do explanatory work.

- Prefer one large image per section.
- Use a row of 2–4 images only when comparison or sequence matters.
- Give an image its own section when the audience should pause and look.
- Add a short caption only when the connection is not obvious.
- Use video for behavior, performance, interaction, sound, or process that a still image cannot show.
- Use bookmarks or embeds for websites, events, references, prototypes, or playable work.
- Keep media close to the idea it supports.
- Never stack many unexplained images in the main presentation path. Move archives into a toggle.

## Text

Write for speaking, not silent reading.

- Use short headings: `Intro`, `What’s New`, `The Idea`, `Timeline`, `Opportunities`, `Next Steps`.
- Use bullets as cues rather than complete narration.
- Bold only the words the presenter or audience needs to find quickly.
- Use a callout for one important question, definition, constraint, or ask.
- Avoid paragraphs longer than 3–4 lines in the main presentation path.
- When a longer explanation is necessary, split it across divider-based sections.

## Useful Section Types

### Opening

Name the project or meeting and give one sentence of orientation. Add a cover image, hero image, or short callout if available.

### Agenda

Use 3–6 bullets. Keep the order aligned with the sections that follow.

### Context

Explain why the work or conversation matters. Use one short paragraph, a quote, or a visual reference.

### Question

Use a callout with the central question. Let it stand alone when discussion is the point.

### Proposal or Idea

State the concept simply. Add one image, video, or embed that makes it tangible.

### Process

Show a sequence, timeline, or a few milestones. Use bullets for a lightweight process and a table only when dates or phases need alignment.

### Roles or Opportunities

Use short callouts or bullets for each role. Avoid long job-description paragraphs.

### Next Steps

End with decisions, owners, deadlines, or the next meeting. Make the requested action obvious.

## Tables

Use tables sparingly. They work best for:

- timelines;
- phases and goals;
- roles and owners;
- options being compared;
- dates and deliverables.

Keep tables to a few columns. If a table needs extensive explanation, turn each major row into its own section instead.

## Toggles and Presenter Notes

Keep the presentation path visible. Use toggles for:

- research and citations;
- raw process documentation;
- long prompts;
- detailed budgets;
- alternate ideas;
- archives and extra media;
- presenter notes or backup material.

Do not hide the central idea, decision, or ask inside a toggle.

## Default Structure

```markdown
Short opening statement or callout.

---

## Agenda

- Topic one
- Topic two
- Topic three

---

## The Idea

One sentence explaining the idea.

![One strong visual](IMAGE)

---

## Why It Matters

- Short point
- Short point
- Short point

---

## Timeline

| Phase | Goal |
| --- | --- |
| Research | Learn and test |
| Build | Create the first version |
| Share | Present and gather feedback |

---

## Next Steps

- Decision needed
- Owner
- Date
```

## Workflow

1. Identify the audience, purpose, and final ask.
2. List the 4–8 ideas the audience needs in order.
3. Give each idea its own divider-based section.
4. Choose the strongest available image, video, embed, callout, bullets, or table for each section.
5. Remove repeated explanation and move backup detail into toggles.
6. Scroll through the page as if presenting. Every section should have one obvious point within a few seconds.
7. Confirm the closing section tells the audience what happens next.

## Final Check

- Does every divider create a meaningful slide-like transition?
- Does every section have one main idea?
- Can each heading be understood immediately?
- Is the text short enough to speak around rather than read aloud?
- Are images large, relevant, and explained only when necessary?
- Are tables limited to information that benefits from alignment?
- Is backup detail hidden without hiding the main argument?
- Does the presentation end with a clear decision, ask, or next step?
- Is the page as simple as possible?
